1. The story in one minute

Bangkok sits on the flat delta of the Chao Phraya river. Most of the city is only a metre or two above sea level, it is crossed by hundreds of canals (khlongs), and parts of it are slowly sinking. Water can arrive three ways: the river rising from the north, high tides pushing up from the Gulf of Thailand, and heavy monsoon rain that the drains can't clear fast enough. The 2011 floods showed how far water can spread across the region when these combine.

This tool asks a simple question: if the water rises to a given height, which parts of Bangkok go under first, and how many people live there? It answers it with free satellite and open data, live, while the audience watches.

Tip: Open with the current flooding. Show one headline or photo from a news source you trust, then say: “Let's see which parts of the city the terrain says are most exposed.” The app's own satellite view of this week's flood (Section 7) is your payoff later in the talk. Check any news figures yourself before quoting them.
Bangkok is one of the lowest-lying major cities in Asia. I built a model that lets us raise the water level and watch, district by district, where the city floods first and who is affected.

2. Before you present

Setup checklist

  1. Install once: pip install -r requirements.txt
  2. Download the data once (about 60 MB, cached in data/processed/): python scripts/prepare_data.py
  3. Start the app: streamlit run app.py. It opens at http://localhost:8501.
  4. Press F11 for full screen. Keep Dark basemap on, because it projects better.
  5. Check your internet connection. The map background tiles stream online. The flood data itself is local.
  6. Do a full run-through once. The first load takes a few seconds, and the animation takes about 10 seconds.
  7. Reset to the starting state before you go on: water level 1.0 m, weights 0.50 / 0.30 / 0.20, layers Flood depth only.

3. Tour of the screen

  1. Water level slider (0–3 m above sea level). The main control of the whole demo.
  2. “Water must flow from rivers/canals”. On: a river or tidal flood (low ground floods only if water can reach it). Off: rain pooling in every low spot.
  3. Animate rising water. Plays 0 → 3 m in about 10 s. This is the moment that grabs the audience.
  4. Risk index weights. How much low ground, nearness to water, and population density count toward risk (Section 5).
  5. Map layers. Flood depth, ground elevation (with an adjustable colour range), risk index, ML flood susceptibility, observed 2011 flood, radar flood (with a date picker), rivers & canals, dark/light basemap.
  6. Headline numbers. Water level, flooded area, people affected (whole map area), and how many of the 50 districts are more than 25% flooded.
  7. Interactive map. Zoom, pan, and hover any district for its details. The layer button is at the top right.

Below the map are eight tabs: Most affected districts, Elevation profile, Flood curve, Current flood, Buildings 3D, ML model, District table and Method & limitations.

5. The risk index and its weights

What the index is

Every ~65 m cell gets a score from 0 to 1 that blends three factors, each scaled from 0 to 1:

FactorHow it's measuredWhat it represents
Low elevationLowest ground in the area scores 1, highest scores 0 (2nd–98th percentile)Hazard: where water collects
Near river / canal1 on a waterway, fading with distance (about 37% at 1 km)Hazard: where overflow reaches first
Population densityLog of people per km², scaled to the densest cellExposure: how many people are in harm's way

Risk = (welev × low elevation + wwater × near water + wpop × density) ÷ (welev + wwater + wpop)

Because the index is divided by the sum of the weights, only the ratios matter: 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2 gives exactly the same map as 1.0 / 0.6 / 0.4. District scores are the average of their cells, and they only mean something relative to each other.

Weights change only the risk layer and the “Risk index” column. They don't change the flood simulation, which depends only on the water level and the connectivity switch. Make this clear so nobody thinks the weights move the water.

Why change the weights? There's no single “correct” setting

Choosing weights is a judgment call, not a law of physics. Here are the reasons to change them, in the order they're most useful in a presentation:

  1. Match the type of flood you're discussing. For a river or tidal flood, raise Near river / canal, because overflow reaches the banks first. For a cloudburst or drainage failure, raise Low elevation and drop Near river / canal, because rain collects in low spots wherever they are. Pair this with the connectivity switch.
  2. Match the question your audience is asking. Engineers and land-use planners care about hazard (where the water goes), so set population to 0. Emergency managers, hospitals and aid groups care about people, so raise population density. The same data answers different questions.
  3. Test how robust the result is (sensitivity analysis). If a district ranks near the top under every reasonable weighting, it's a strong priority, and nobody can say you picked weights to get your answer. Suan Luang ranks #1 in 4 of the 5 scenarios below.
  4. Allow for weaknesses in the data. The elevation data includes buildings, so it overstates height in the dense centre. Leaning heavily on elevation can therefore understate central risk. OpenStreetMap's canal mapping is more complete in some areas than others. If you distrust a factor, lower its weight and say why.
  5. Calibrate against real floods. Once you have an observed flood map (for example from satellite radar), you can pick the weights that best reproduce it. This turns opinion into evidence. This project now does it (Section 6), and the result is striking: against the 2011 flood, the default weights score below random.
  6. Reflect stakeholder or policy priorities. A city might decide that protecting people counts twice as much as protecting land. Weights are where values enter a model, so it's better to make them visible and adjustable than hidden. (Formal methods such as pairwise-comparison surveys exist for setting them with stakeholders.)
  7. Explain what drives risk. Setting one weight to 1 and the others to 0 shows each factor on its own. It's a quick way to show the audience what each ingredient contributes.

What to point out

  • Suan Luang and Bang Kapi stay near the top in 4 of 5 settings. They're low, cut through by canals, and densely populated, so they're robust priorities.

The weights are where judgment enters the model, so I've made them a slider instead of hiding them. Whatever you care about, river floods or rain, land or people, a few districts come out on top every time. Those are the ones to act on first.

6. The machine-learning model

The risk index uses weights that we chose. The machine-learning model asks a different question: given where Bangkok actually flooded in 2011, which combination of terrain and land cover best explains it?

How it was built

  • What really flooded: the Global Flood Database's map of the 2011 flood (event DFO_3850, 5 Aug 2011 – 9 Jan 2012), made from MODIS satellite images at 250 m. That's 846,343 grid cells marked flooded or dry, and 21% of them flooded.
  • What the model sees: elevation, height above the nearest waterway, elevation compared with the surroundings, slope, distance to any canal and to the Chao Phraya, and land cover (cropland, water, trees, grassland, wetland) from ESA WorldCover.
  • Model: LightGBM, a gradient-boosted tree model that works well on this kind of table-style data.
  • Fair test: the map is cut into 4 km blocks, and each method is scored only on blocks the model never saw. That stops it from “cheating” by memorising neighbouring cells.
Method (scored on unseen blocks)ROC-AUCPR-AUC
ML model0.870.67
Bathtub simulation0.670.29
Hand-weighted risk index (default weights)0.450.18
Random guess0.500.21

ROC-AUC: how often the method ranks a flooded cell above a dry one (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect). PR-AUC: how well it finds flooded cells without false alarms. A random guess scores the flooded share (0.21).

Show it on the map

What to point out

  • The ML model beats both existing methods by a wide margin (ROC-AUC 0.87 vs 0.67 and 0.45). At district level, its score correlates 0.72 with the share of each district that flooded.
  • The hand-weighted risk index scored below random. It ranks dense, canal-rich inner districts highest, but in 2011 those were protected by flood walls and pumping and stayed dry. It's the strongest argument for testing a model against reality.
  • What the model learned: low ground and cropland flooded, and being closer to the Chao Phraya raised the chance. Farmland on the city's edge absorbed the flood while the protected core stayed dry.
  • A judgment call made visible: population density and built-up land were left out on purpose. With them the model scored higher (0.89), but its main lesson became “dense city = dry”. That mostly reflects defences, plus the satellite's difficulty seeing water between buildings, and it would be a dangerous message in a flood tool.
The weights I picked by hand pointed at the wrong places. They scored worse than a coin flip against what actually happened in 2011. A model that learned from the real flood ranks flooded places above dry ones about 87% of the time. That's why you test models against reality.
Be honest about the limits: the 2011 map is 250 m resolution and under-counts flooding in dense urban areas. Some coastal “flooding” is shrimp and fish ponds. It's one river flood, so it teaches nothing about cloudbursts. The model also misses some places: Don Mueang, whose airport famously flooded in 2011 (21% of the district), scores only 0.27.

7. The current flood

Sentinel-1 radar satellites pass over Bangkok every 4–8 days. Radar sees through cloud, and calm open water reflects the signal away from the satellite, so it shows up dark. For each pass, the app marks water that is unusual for the time of year: cells that are dark now and much darker than the same weeks last year, seen from the same orbit, with permanent rivers and ponds removed.

Before you present: the figures below are from the 27 Sep 2026 pass. To add passes since then, re-run python scripts/fetch_current_flood.py --gee-project YOUR_PROJECT and python scripts/evaluate_current.py, and check the numbers in the app before quoting them.

The story in three numbers

  • About 200 mm of rain in three days (about 122 mm on 25 Sep alone, weather-model estimate), on top of a Chao Phraya already running about 15% above normal. By the 27 Sep pass the river was at about 130% of normal for the date (GloFAS river model).
  • 99 km² of unusual water across greater Bangkok on 27 Sep, up from 27 km² on 19 Sep. 32 km² of it is inside the city's 50 districts.
  • Most flooded districts: Nong Chok 8.3% of its area, Lat Krabang 5.9%, Min Buri 2.8%, Khlong Sam Wa 2.0%. Nong Chok was also the most flooded district in 2011.
Two days of extreme rain, and the water went exactly where the terrain says it would: the low farmland in the east. Nong Chok tops the list this week, just as it did in 2011.
And the river was already running high before the rain started, so the water had less room to drain away.

8. Buildings in 3D

The Buildings 3D tab turns flooded area into something people recognise: 2,116,261 buildings across Bangkok's 50 districts, each with its footprint and height, coloured by whether the water reaches it. Footprints and heights come from Google Open Buildings, which is mapped from satellite imagery by AI. Hospitals, clinics, schools, universities, police and fire stations come from OpenStreetMap.

How to use it

  • View: Whole city for the overview, District for individual 3D buildings. The district list is sorted by how many buildings are affected.
  • Colour buildings by: the simulated water level, this week's radar flood, the ML model, or the 2011 flood. It follows the sidebar, so moving the water-level slider updates the buildings.
  • Height exaggeration: Bangkok is mostly low-rise. Stretching heights 2–3× makes the 3D easier to read.
  • Building shapes: districts over 40,000 buildings open in Light mode (each building a small column at its true height), which loads much faster. Switch to Footprints for true shapes, which takes up to about 30 seconds in the largest districts.
  • Camera: the district view opens on the flooded area. Scroll to zoom, right-drag (or Ctrl-drag) to tilt and rotate, and hover a building for its height and flood value.

10. Anticipated questions

QuestionSuggested answer
Is this a forecast of the current flood?No. It's a “what-if” tool: it shows where water would go at a given height. The Current flood tab shows recent rainfall and modelled river flow for context, but they don't set the water level. Driving the simulation from live river gauges is a planned next step.
Why doesn't central Bangkok flood more?Two reasons. The elevation data includes buildings, which makes the dense core look higher than the ground really is. In reality, the centre is also protected by flood walls, dikes and pumps, which this model doesn't include. Treat the centre's result with caution.
Why does the flooded area jump at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 m?That's a quirk of the elevation dataset, not a real threshold. Copernicus rounds many flat areas to 0.5 m heights, so big patches switch to flooded together. Look at the overall trend.
Why are the headline numbers bigger than the table totals?The headline cards cover the whole map area (greater Bangkok, about 18 M people). The table and district counts cover only Bangkok's 50 districts (about 10.9 M).
How accurate is the elevation?It's a 30 m global satellite surface model, good for relative comparisons across the city. Treat differences smaller than about half a metre, and any single street, as uncertain.
Who chose the weights? Aren't they arbitrary?They're a transparent judgment, and that's why they're sliders. Show the five scenarios: the top districts stay much the same, so the conclusion doesn't depend on the exact weights. Calibrating against observed floods is the next step.
Where does the data come from, and how current is it?Copernicus GLO-30 elevation (satellite radar), WorldPop 2020 population estimates, OpenStreetMap rivers and canals, and geoBoundaries districts. All are free and open.
Why stop at 3 m?By 3 m most of the low-lying region is under water, and the bathtub assumption becomes unrealistic for a flood that big.
Is the machine-learning model better than the risk index?Against the real 2011 flood, yes, by far: 0.87 vs 0.45 ROC-AUC, tested on areas it never saw. But it has only seen one flood. It's the best evidence we have, not a guarantee.
Why did the hand-weighted index do worse than random?It rates dense, canal-rich inner districts highest. In 2011 those were protected by walls and pumps and stayed dry, while farmland on the edge flooded. Hand-set weights can be confidently wrong, which is why testing matters.
Why does being farther from canals raise the ML flood chance?Canals are densest in the protected city, and the fields that flooded in 2011 are farther apart. The model is picking up “protected city vs open farmland”, not canals keeping places dry. That's a correlation, not a cause.
Why leave population out of the ML model?With it, the model learned “dense city = dry”, mostly because of flood defences and the satellite missing urban floodwater. That's a dangerous message for a flood tool, and removing it cost little accuracy (0.89 → 0.87).
Is the radar map the official flood extent?No. It's our own analysis of public satellite images, useful for patterns across the city. For official figures, refer people to the Bangkok Metropolitan Administration or Thailand's disaster agencies.
Why does the radar show almost no flooding in the city centre?Partly because the centre is better drained and protected, and partly because radar can't see water between tall buildings well. Treat “no flooding” in the dense core as “not detected”, not “dry”.
Is the river flow a gauge reading?No. It comes from GloFAS, a global river model, and it reads high on the Chao Phraya. It's useful for comparing with the river's normal for the date, not for exact figures. For official river levels, refer people to the Royal Irrigation Department.
Isn't this just rice paddies?We compare against the same weeks last year, so paddies flooded on the usual schedule mostly cancel out. The jump came right after about 200 mm of rain, which points to real flooding, but some field-management changes can still slip through.
Are these real buildings?Yes. They're Google's AI-mapped footprints from satellite imagery, with heights from the same project. They're good at city scale, but individual buildings can be wrong, merged or missing.
Does “affected” mean the building floods inside?No. It means the building stands in a ~65 m area that floods in that scenario. Raised ground floors, walls and pumps can keep water out, so treat it as “in the flood zone”.
Can I zoom in to my street?You can zoom the map, but the model works on ~65 m cells, so it's meant for district-scale patterns, not individual buildings.

11. Limitations and next steps

Limitations (say these out loud, because it builds credibility)

  • “Bathtub” model: water is static. There's no flow speed, rainfall timing, tide cycle or flood duration.
  • No flood defences: walls, dikes, pumping stations, drainage tunnels and water gates are not modelled.
  • Surface elevation: buildings and trees inflate heights, especially in the centre. Ground subsidence of a few cm per year isn't included.
  • Elevation rounding: many flat areas are stored in 0.5 m steps, which makes the curve jump at those levels.
  • Population: 2020 modelled estimates of where people live, not where they are during the day.
  • Risk index: relative, not absolute. It has no vulnerability factor (for example income, building type, or access to hospitals). Against 2011 it scored below random.
  • ML model: trained on one river flood with 250 m labels that under-count urban flooding. It reflects 2011-era defences and says nothing about cloudbursts.
  • Radar flood maps: under-count water between buildings, can mistake smooth surfaces or field flooding for floodwater, and are snapshots every 4–8 days.
  • Buildings: AI-estimated footprints and heights; “affected” is judged per ~65 m cell, not per building; small sheds left out in the six largest districts.

Next steps

  1. More floods for the model: use this month's radar flood maps as a second training event, so the model learns from both a river flood (2011) and a rain flood (2026).
  2. Go live: rainfall and modelled river flow are now shown next to each radar pass; the next step is using real river-gauge levels to set the simulated water level.
  3. Add defences: include the known flood walls and dikes as barriers in the connectivity step.
  4. Add vulnerability: hospitals, schools and income data to go beyond exposure.
  5. Share it: deploy on Streamlit Community Cloud so anyone can open a link.
